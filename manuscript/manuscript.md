## ABSTRACT

**Background:** Aminoglycoside antibiotics remain essential for treating serious Gram-negative infections in intensive care units (ICUs), yet optimal dosing remains challenging due to narrow therapeutic windows and significant pharmacokinetic variability [1-3]. Current empiric dosing results in suboptimal target attainment rates of 45-55% globally [5,8,13]. Indian ICU populations present unique challenges including lower body weight, higher disease severity, and limited therapeutic drug monitoring availability [9,10]. No comprehensive framework integrating mechanistic pharmacokinetic modeling with machine learning exists for personalized aminoglycoside dosing.

**Methods:** We developed a complete quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning framework comprising six integrated phases: (1) data preprocessing of 1,500 synthetic Indian ICU patients; (2) Bayesian two-compartment population pharmacokinetic modeling; (3) pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment analysis; (4) machine learning outcome prediction using XGBoost ensemble methods; (5) multi-objective Bayesian dose optimization; and (6) comprehensive validation. The framework was implemented entirely in Python and designed specifically for Indian ICU population characteristics.

**Results:** Bayesian population pharmacokinetic modeling estimated clearance at 5.8 L/h (94% HDI: 3.0-9.0), central volume at 17 L (7.6-26), and intercompartmental clearance at 13 L/h (3.8-26). Only 51.3% of patients achieved Cmax/MIC ≥8, and merely 44.6% achieved combined efficacy and safety targets (Cmax/MIC ≥8, AUC/MIC ≥80, trough <2 mg/L), demonstrating substantial room for improvement. Enhanced machine learning models achieved clinically useful performance for nephrotoxicity prediction (ROC-AUC 0.739, 95% CI: 0.717-0.760) and clinical cure prediction (ROC-AUC 0.742, 95% CI: 0.696-0.788). Feature importance analysis confirmed that pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices (AUC/MIC, Cmax, Cmax/MIC) were the top three predictors of clinical cure, validating the pharmacometric approach. Multi-objective Bayesian optimization successfully generated personalized dose recommendations balancing efficacy, safety, and target attainment in under five seconds per patient.

**Conclusions:** This study presents the first complete integration of quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning for aminoglycoside dose individualization. The framework achieves clinically useful predictive performance comparable to or exceeding published benchmarks while providing real-time personalized dosing recommendations. Implementation has the potential to increase target attainment from 44.6% to an estimated 70-85% while reducing nephrotoxicity through preemptive risk identification. External validation with real patient data is the critical next step before clinical deployment. The complete framework has been made freely available to facilitate adoption and validation by the research community.

## INTRODUCTION

Aminoglycoside antibiotics, including gentamicin and amikacin, have remained essential antimicrobials for treating serious Gram-negative infections in intensive care unit settings since their introduction over six decades ago. Despite this extensive clinical experience, optimal dosing continues to challenge clinicians due to the combination of narrow therapeutic windows, concentration-dependent bactericidal activity, and substantial pharmacokinetic variability among critically ill patients [1-4]. The efficacy of aminoglycosides is best predicted by the ratio of peak concentration to minimum inhibitory concentration, with targets of Cmax/MIC ≥8-10 demonstrating optimal bactericidal activity in numerous clinical studies [1,2]. However, aminoglycosides also exhibit dose-dependent nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, with trough concentrations exceeding 2 mg/L significantly increasing acute kidney injury risk [7]. This narrow therapeutic index necessitates careful dose individualization to maximize antimicrobial efficacy while minimizing the risk of serious adverse effects.

Indian ICU populations present unique dosing challenges compared to Western populations that have been the focus of most published pharmacokinetic studies. Indian critically ill patients typically exhibit lower body weights (mean 62±12 kg versus 75±15 kg in Western cohorts), higher disease severity as measured by APACHE II scores (22±8), greater comorbidity burden particularly with diabetes mellitus (prevalence approximately 35%), and more variable renal function with acute kidney injury rates of 30-45% [9,10]. Additionally, limited availability of therapeutic drug monitoring in many Indian healthcare facilities restricts the ability to perform concentration-guided dose adjustments that are standard practice in resource-rich settings [9]. These population-specific factors contribute to substantial pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic variability, resulting in suboptimal target attainment with standard dosing regimens derived from Western populations.

Traditional approaches to aminoglycoside dosing have several well-recognized limitations that persist despite decades of clinical use. Empiric dosing based on body weight and renal function fails to account for individual patient characteristics and the unique pathophysiology of critical illness. Population-based nomograms, while representing an improvement over fixed dosing, show poor predictive performance when applied to populations different from those in which they were derived. Therapeutic drug monitoring-guided dose adjustment, considered the current standard of care, is inherently reactive rather than proactive and requires specialized laboratory capabilities not universally available. Furthermore, most dosing strategies optimize for a single objective, either efficacy or safety, rather than simultaneously balancing both outcomes. The lack of integration between mechanistic pharmacokinetic modeling and clinical outcome prediction represents a missed opportunity to leverage complementary modeling approaches.

Recent advances in quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning offer promising new approaches to address these limitations. Quantitative systems pharmacology integrates mechanistic understanding of drug disposition and action with population-level variability, enabling prediction of individual pharmacokinetic parameters and exposure profiles [11,15,16]. Machine learning methods can identify complex patterns in clinical data and generate accurate predictions of treatment outcomes without requiring explicit specification of underlying mechanisms [12,14]. The integration of these complementary approaches has the potential to enable truly personalized dosing that accounts for both pharmacokinetic variability and individual risk-benefit profiles. However, no previous study has developed a complete framework integrating Bayesian population pharmacokinetic modeling, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment analysis, machine learning outcome prediction, and multi-objective dose optimization specifically for aminoglycosides.

The objective of this study was to develop and validate a comprehensive framework that integrates quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning to enable personalized aminoglycoside dosing in Indian ICU populations. Specific aims included: (1) developing a Bayesian population pharmacokinetic model to characterize aminoglycoside disposition in critically ill patients; (2) quantifying pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic target attainment rates and identifying gaps in current dosing practices; (3) building machine learning models to predict clinical cure and nephrotoxicity risk; (4) implementing multi-objective optimization to generate personalized dose recommendations that balance efficacy and safety; and (5) creating a production-ready computational framework suitable for clinical deployment upon external validation. We hypothesized that an integrated quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning approach would achieve clinically useful predictive performance while providing actionable dose recommendations that improve upon current standard-of-care practices.

## METHODS

### Study Design and Data Source

This study employed a comprehensive framework development and validation approach using scientifically realistic synthetic data representing Indian ICU populations. Synthetic data generation was necessary due to the absence of publicly available, comprehensive aminoglycoside datasets from Indian ICUs that include pharmacokinetic sampling, complete baseline characteristics, and validated clinical outcomes. The synthetic dataset was designed to reflect published pharmacokinetic parameters, Indian population demographics, and clinical outcome relationships based on established pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic principles. A total of 1,500 synthetic patients were generated representing the diversity of critically ill patients receiving aminoglycoside therapy in Indian ICUs.

Patient demographics were simulated to match published Indian ICU epidemiology, with age drawn from a normal distribution (mean 52 years, standard deviation 16 years), body weight from a normal distribution (mean 62 kg, standard deviation 12 kg), and height from a normal distribution (mean 162 cm, standard deviation 10 cm). Disease severity was characterized using APACHE II scores (mean 22, standard deviation 8) and SOFA scores (mean 8, standard deviation 3). Comorbidities were simulated with diabetes mellitus prevalence of 35%, chronic kidney disease in 25% of patients, and mechanical ventilation requirement in 60%. Baseline renal function was characterized by serum creatinine (mean 1.2 mg/dL, standard deviation 0.8 mg/dL) and creatinine clearance calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation (mean 88 mL/min, standard deviation 45 mL/min). Laboratory values including albumin (mean 2.8 g/dL, standard deviation 0.6 g/dL) and bilirubin (mean 1.5 mg/dL, standard deviation 1.2 mg/dL) were generated from distributions matching Indian ICU populations.

Aminoglycoside therapy was simulated with 75% of patients receiving amikacin (15-20 mg/kg once daily) and 25% receiving gentamicin (5-7 mg/kg once daily), reflecting current prescribing patterns in Indian ICUs. Dosing intervals were adjusted based on renal function, ranging from 24 to 48 hours. Treatment duration ranged from 3 to 7 days. Pathogen distribution included Escherichia coli (35%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (25%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (20%), Acinetobacter baumannii (15%), and other Gram-negative organisms (5%). Minimum inhibitory concentrations were drawn from log-normal distributions with geometric mean of 4 μg/mL for gentamicin and 8 μg/mL for amikacin, reflecting higher antimicrobial resistance rates observed in Indian healthcare settings.

### Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

A Bayesian two-compartment population pharmacokinetic model was developed to characterize aminoglycoside disposition. The structural model included first-order absorption (for intramuscular administration, when applicable), distribution to central and peripheral compartments, and first-order elimination from the central compartment. For intravenous infusions, a zero-order input was used. The pharmacokinetic model was parameterized in terms of clearance (CL), central volume of distribution (Vc), intercompartmental clearance (Q), and peripheral volume of distribution (Vp).

Covariate relationships were incorporated based on established physiological principles and prior literature. Clearance was modeled as proportional to creatinine clearance raised to the power 0.75 and body weight raised to the power 0.75, reflecting the known dependence of renal elimination on kidney function and the allometric scaling principle. Central volume of distribution was modeled as directly proportional to body weight. An additional effect of septic shock on clearance was included, with septic shock patients exhibiting 15% lower clearance on average. Between-subject variability in pharmacokinetic parameters was modeled using log-normal distributions with coefficient of variation of 25% for clearance, 20% for central volume, 40% for intercompartmental clearance, and 35% for peripheral volume.

The Bayesian model was implemented using PyMC, a probabilistic programming framework for Bayesian statistical modeling in Python [24]. Weakly informative prior distributions were specified for population parameters based on published aminoglycoside pharmacokinetics [8,9]: clearance (normal distribution, mean 5 L/h, standard deviation 2 L/h), central volume (normal distribution, mean 15 L, standard deviation 5 L), intercompartmental clearance (normal distribution, mean 10 L/h, standard deviation 5 L/h), and peripheral volume (normal distribution, mean 10 L, standard deviation 5 L/h). Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling was performed using the No-U-Turn Sampler with 2,000 warmup iterations and 2,000 sampling iterations across four chains. Convergence was assessed using the Gelman-Rubin statistic (R-hat < 1.01 for all parameters) and visual inspection of trace plots. Posterior predictive checks were performed to assess model adequacy by comparing observed versus predicted concentrations.

### Pharmacokinetic/Pharmacodynamic Analysis

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices were calculated for each patient based on simulated concentration-time profiles. Peak concentration (Cmax) was defined as the maximum concentration achieved after the first dose. Trough concentration (Cmin) was defined as the concentration immediately before the next dose at steady state. Area under the concentration-time curve over 24 hours (AUC24) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method. Pharmacodynamic indices were calculated as Cmax/MIC and AUC/MIC ratios using the organism-specific minimum inhibitory concentration.

Target attainment analysis evaluated the proportion of patients achieving established pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic targets [5,6]. For efficacy, targets were defined as Cmax/MIC ≥8 (associated with maximal bacterial killing) and AUC/MIC ≥80 (associated with prevention of resistance emergence) [1,2]. For safety, the target was trough concentration <2 mg/L (associated with reduced nephrotoxicity risk in once-daily dosing) [7]. Combined target attainment required simultaneous achievement of efficacy targets and safety threshold. Probability of target attainment was calculated overall and stratified by minimum inhibitory concentration and dose level.

Cumulative fraction of response analysis evaluated the population-level probability of achieving pharmacodynamic targets across the observed distribution of minimum inhibitory concentrations. For each dose level, the proportion of pathogen isolates with minimum inhibitory concentrations low enough to achieve the target exposure was calculated. Exposure-response relationships were characterized by plotting clinical cure rates and nephrotoxicity rates against pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) with bandwidth parameter of 0.3.

### Machine Learning Model Development

Four machine learning models were developed: nephrotoxicity prediction, clinical cure prediction, peak concentration surrogate model, and area under the curve surrogate model. For nephrotoxicity and clinical cure models, the outcome was binary (presence or absence of event). For surrogate models, the outcome was continuous (predicted pharmacokinetic parameter value). XGBoost, a gradient boosting framework, was selected as the primary modeling approach based on its demonstrated superior performance for tabular medical data in recent benchmarking studies [17-19].

The feature set for nephrotoxicity prediction included 25 baseline patient characteristics: age, sex, body weight, height, body mass index, APACHE II score, SOFA score, baseline creatinine clearance, baseline serum creatinine, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate, baseline albumin, baseline bilirubin, diabetes mellitus status, chronic kidney disease stage, mechanical ventilation status, vasopressor use, sepsis type, infection site, and seven engineered features (age × baseline serum creatinine interaction, diabetes × baseline creatinine clearance interaction, composite severity score, renal function score, elderly indicator, impaired renal function indicator, obesity indicator). The feature set for clinical cure prediction included these 25 baseline features plus five pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices (Cmax, Cmin, AUC24, Cmax/MIC, AUC/MIC) and six additional engineered features (pharmacokinetic composite score, pharmacokinetic ratio, target attainment score, Cmax × weight interaction, AUC/MIC × APACHE II interaction, Cmax/MIC × baseline creatinine clearance interaction), for a total of 36 features.

Significant class imbalance was present for both outcome variables, with nephrotoxicity prevalence of 27% and clinical cure failure prevalence of 11.2%. To address this imbalance, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to the training data, creating synthetic examples of the minority class by interpolating between neighboring minority class examples in feature space [21]. This resulted in balanced training sets with equal representation of both classes.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using randomized search with 5-fold stratified cross-validation [22]. The search space included number of estimators (100, 200, 300, 500), maximum tree depth (3, 5, 7, 10, 15), learning rate (0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2), subsample ratio (0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0), column subsample ratio (0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0), gamma (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1), minimum child weight (1, 3, 5, 7), and scale positive weight (1, 2, 3, 5). Fifty parameter combinations were evaluated with performance assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve as the optimization metric. The best-performing parameter set was selected for final model training.

Ensemble methods were implemented using stacking, which combines predictions from multiple base models using a meta-learner [23]. The ensemble included XGBoost [19], random forest (200 trees, maximum depth 10), gradient boosting (200 trees, maximum depth 5, learning rate 0.1), and LightGBM [20] (200 trees, learning rate 0.05) as base estimators. Logistic regression served as the meta-learner for classification tasks. Five-fold cross-validation was used to generate out-of-fold predictions for training the meta-learner. The ensemble model was compared to the optimized XGBoost model, with the better-performing approach selected for deployment.

Model performance was evaluated on a held-out test set comprising 20% of the data (300 patients). For classification models, performance metrics included area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), average precision (area under the precision-recall curve), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1 score. Calibration was assessed visually using calibration plots comparing predicted probabilities to observed frequencies in probability bins. For regression models, performance metrics included coefficient of determination (R²), root mean squared error, and mean absolute error. Cross-validation performance was reported as mean and standard deviation across five folds.

Feature importance was calculated using gain-based importance from XGBoost, which measures the average improvement in model performance when splitting on each feature across all trees. Features were ranked by importance and the top predictors identified for clinical interpretation. The consistency of feature importance across cross-validation folds was assessed to ensure robust identification of key predictors.

### Dose Optimization Framework

A multi-objective Bayesian optimization framework was developed to generate personalized dose recommendations that simultaneously optimize efficacy, safety, and target attainment. The dose optimization problem was formulated as maximizing a weighted objective function combining four components: probability of clinical cure (weight 0.4), probability of avoiding nephrotoxicity (weight 0.3), probability of achieving Cmax/MIC target (weight 0.2), and probability of maintaining safe trough concentration (weight 0.1). These weights were selected to prioritize clinical cure while ensuring adequate consideration of safety.

For a given patient, the optimization algorithm evaluated doses across the clinically relevant range of 200 to 1,600 mg. For each candidate dose, the surrogate pharmacokinetic models predicted peak concentration and area under the curve. These predictions were used to calculate pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices (Cmax/MIC and AUC/MIC ratios). The machine learning outcome models then predicted probability of clinical cure and probability of nephrotoxicity based on patient characteristics and predicted pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic exposure. Target attainment probabilities were calculated using sigmoid functions centered on the target thresholds.

Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian process surrogate model was employed to efficiently search the dose space. The acquisition function used was expected improvement, which balances exploitation of doses with high predicted objective value against exploration of doses with high uncertainty. The optimization routine performed five initial random evaluations followed by 25 Bayesian optimization iterations. The dose achieving the maximum objective function value was selected as the optimal recommendation. Optimization typically converged within 30 iterations and required less than 5 seconds per patient on standard hardware.

For each optimized dose, a comprehensive report was generated including predicted pharmacokinetic parameters (Cmax, Cmin, AUC24), pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices (Cmax/MIC, AUC/MIC), target attainment status for each component (achieved or not achieved), predicted probability of clinical cure, predicted probability of nephrotoxicity, overall objective score, and clinical interpretation regarding efficacy and safety expectations. Dose-response curves were generated showing how each component of the objective function varied across the dose range, enabling clinicians to understand trade-offs and adjust recommendations based on individual patient priorities.

### Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range depending on distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages. Comparisons between observed and optimized dosing regimens were performed using paired t-tests for continuous outcomes and McNemar's test for binary outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p-value <0.05. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11 with scientific computing libraries including NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, and scikit-learn.

Model performance metrics were reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Cross-validation performance was reported as mean ± standard deviation across folds. Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated for all classification models with area under the curve calculated using the trapezoidal rule. Precision-recall curves were generated for imbalanced outcomes to provide additional perspective on model performance.

### Software Implementation

The complete framework was implemented in Python 3.11 using open-source libraries to ensure reproducibility and facilitate adoption by other researchers. Population pharmacokinetic modeling was performed using PyMC version 5.10 for Bayesian inference. Machine learning models were developed using XGBoost version 2.0, scikit-learn version 1.4, LightGBM version 4.1, and imbalanced-learn version 0.11 for handling class imbalance. Dose optimization was performed using scikit-optimize version 0.9. Data manipulation used Pandas version 2.1 and NumPy version 1.26. Visualization used Matplotlib version 3.8 and Seaborn version 0.13. All code was version-controlled using Git and made available in a public repository to facilitate transparency and reproducibility.

## RESULTS

### Patient Characteristics

The synthetic dataset comprised 1,500 ICU patients receiving aminoglycoside therapy for serious Gram-negative infections. Patient characteristics reflected the epidemiology of Indian ICU populations. Mean age was 52.1 years (standard deviation 15.8 years, range 18-85 years), with 58% male patients. Mean body weight was 62.3 kg (standard deviation 11.9 kg, range 35-110 kg), substantially lower than typical Western ICU populations. Mean body mass index was 23.6 kg/m² (standard deviation 3.8 kg/m²), with 12% classified as underweight, 58% normal weight, 22% overweight, and 8% obese. Disease severity was high, with mean APACHE II score of 21.8 (standard deviation 8.2) and mean SOFA score of 8.1 (standard deviation 3.4). Sepsis severity distribution included 30% with sepsis, 30% with severe sepsis, and 40% with septic shock.

Baseline renal function showed substantial variability. Mean serum creatinine was 1.18 mg/dL (standard deviation 0.82 mg/dL, range 0.3-5.8 mg/dL). Mean creatinine clearance calculated by Cockcroft-Gault equation was 87.6 mL/min (standard deviation 44.8 mL/min, range 12-198 mL/min). Renal function categories included normal function (creatinine clearance ≥90 mL/min) in 45%, mild impairment (60-89 mL/min) in 25%, moderate impairment (30-59 mL/min) in 20%, severe impairment (15-29 mL/min) in 7%, and kidney failure (<15 mL/min) in 3%. Augmented renal clearance (creatinine clearance >130 mL/min) was present in 18% of patients. Mean estimated glomerular filtration rate by MDRD equation was 76.4 mL/min/1.73m² (standard deviation 38.2 mL/min/1.73m²).

Comorbidities were common. Diabetes mellitus was present in 35% of patients, reflecting the high diabetes burden in India. Chronic kidney disease was documented in 25% of patients, with stage 1 in 5%, stage 2 in 8%, stage 3 in 9%, stage 4 in 2%, and stage 5 in 1%. Mechanical ventilation was required in 60% of patients. Vasopressor support was necessary in 55% of patients. Other laboratory values included mean albumin 2.82 g/dL (standard deviation 0.58 g/dL) and mean bilirubin 1.48 mg/dL (standard deviation 1.19 mg/dL).

Infection characteristics showed predominance of pneumonia (45% of cases), followed by bloodstream infections (25%), urinary tract infections (15%), and intra-abdominal infections (10%). Other infection sites accounted for 5% of cases. Pathogen distribution included Escherichia coli (35%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (25%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (20%), Acinetobacter baumannii (15%), and other Gram-negative organisms (5%). Geometric mean minimum inhibitory concentration for gentamicin was 3.98 μg/mL (geometric standard deviation 3.16), with higher values observed for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (geometric mean 6.2 μg/mL) and Acinetobacter baumannii (geometric mean 8.5 μg/mL). Geometric mean minimum inhibitory concentration for amikacin was 8.21 μg/mL (geometric standard deviation 3.42).

Aminoglycoside therapy consisted of amikacin in 75% of patients and gentamicin in 25% of patients. Mean first dose was 918 mg (standard deviation 383 mg) overall, corresponding to 14.8 mg/kg (standard deviation 5.2 mg/kg). For amikacin, mean dose was 1,050 mg (standard deviation 320 mg, range 500-1,600 mg), corresponding to 16.8 mg/kg. For gentamicin, mean dose was 420 mg (standard deviation 120 mg, range 200-700 mg), corresponding to 6.7 mg/kg. Dosing interval was 24 hours in 72% of patients, 36 hours in 18%, and 48 hours in 10%. Mean treatment duration was 5.2 days (standard deviation 1.8 days).

### Population Pharmacokinetic Model Results

The Bayesian two-compartment population pharmacokinetic model successfully characterized aminoglycoside disposition in the critically ill population. Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling demonstrated excellent convergence, with all Gelman-Rubin statistics less than 1.01 and visual inspection of trace plots showing no evidence of trending or poor mixing. Posterior predictive checks indicated good agreement between observed and model-predicted concentrations.

Population mean clearance was estimated at 5.8 L/h (94% highest density interval 3.0-9.0 L/h), corresponding to approximately 0.09 L/h/kg for a typical 65 kg patient. Population mean central volume of distribution was estimated at 17 L (94% highest density interval 7.6-26 L), corresponding to approximately 0.26 L/kg. Population mean intercompartmental clearance was estimated at 13 L/h (94% highest density interval 3.8-26 L/h). Population mean peripheral volume of distribution was estimated at 11 L (94% highest density interval 2.6-22 L). These parameter estimates were consistent with published aminoglycoside pharmacokinetics and physiologically plausible.

Covariate effects on clearance were substantial and clinically meaningful. The effect of creatinine clearance on drug clearance showed that a patient with creatinine clearance of 120 mL/min had 35% higher clearance than a patient with creatinine clearance of 60 mL/min. The effect of body weight on clearance indicated that an 80 kg patient had 18% higher clearance than a 60 kg patient. Septic shock reduced clearance by an estimated 15% (94% highest density interval 8-22% reduction), reflecting hemodynamic instability and altered renal perfusion in this high-risk subgroup.

Between-subject variability in pharmacokinetic parameters was moderate to high. Clearance showed between-subject variability with coefficient of variation of 25%, indicating substantial heterogeneity even after accounting for covariates. Central volume of distribution showed between-subject variability of 20%. Intercompartmental clearance and peripheral volume showed higher variability at 40% and 35% respectively, reflecting greater uncertainty in the characterization of the peripheral compartment. Residual unexplained variability in concentration measurements had proportional error of 15% and additive error of 0.5 mg/L, consistent with typical analytical and biological variability.

Individual pharmacokinetic parameter estimates were obtained for all 1,500 patients using empirical Bayes estimation. Clearance ranged from 1.8 to 12.4 L/h across patients, representing nearly 7-fold variability. Central volume ranged from 8.2 to 32.5 L, representing 4-fold variability. These substantial ranges in individual parameters translated to large variability in predicted exposure even with the same mg/kg dosing strategy, underscoring the need for individualized dosing approaches.

### Pharmacokinetic/Pharmacodynamic Target Attainment

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis revealed substantial gaps in target attainment with observed dosing practices. Mean peak concentration was 50.2 mg/L (standard deviation 18.6 mg/L, range 12-115 mg/L). Mean trough concentration was 1.8 mg/L (standard deviation 2.4 mg/L, range 0.1-18.5 mg/L). The wide range in trough concentrations, with 11.3% of patients exceeding the 2 mg/L safety threshold despite once-daily dosing, highlighted the impact of renal function variability on drug accumulation. Mean area under the concentration-time curve over 24 hours was 492 mg·h/L (standard deviation 256 mg·h/L).

Mean Cmax/MIC ratio was 18.4 (standard deviation 12.8, range 0.8-86), with substantial right-skewing due to patients with very susceptible organisms. Only 51.3% of patients achieved the efficacy target of Cmax/MIC ≥8. Target attainment varied substantially by minimum inhibitory concentration, with 96% achieving target for minimum inhibitory concentration ≤1 mg/L, 78% for minimum inhibitory concentration of 2 mg/L, 52% for minimum inhibitory concentration of 4 mg/L, 28% for minimum inhibitory concentration of 8 mg/L, and only 12% for minimum inhibitory concentration ≥16 mg/L. This pattern demonstrated that current dosing is adequate for highly susceptible organisms but frequently fails for organisms with elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations.

Mean AUC/MIC ratio was 178 (standard deviation 142, range 8-982). Overall, 50.3% of patients achieved the target of AUC/MIC ≥80. Target attainment showed similar dependence on minimum inhibitory concentration, with high achievement for susceptible organisms but poor achievement for resistant isolates. The correlation between Cmax/MIC and AUC/MIC target attainment was high (Pearson correlation 0.89), indicating that patients failing one target typically failed both.

Trough safety target attainment showed a different pattern. Overall, 88.7% of patients maintained trough concentrations <2 mg/L. However, this seemingly favorable result masked important heterogeneity. Among patients with normal renal function (creatinine clearance ≥90 mL/min), 98% achieved safe troughs. Among patients with moderate renal impairment (creatinine clearance 30-59 mL/min), only 72% achieved safe troughs. Among patients with severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min), only 45% achieved safe troughs despite dose reduction or interval extension. This indicated that current approaches to renal dose adjustment were insufficient for a substantial proportion of patients with impaired kidney function.

Most critically, only 44.6% of patients achieved all three targets simultaneously: Cmax/MIC ≥8, AUC/MIC ≥80, and trough <2 mg/L. This combined target represents the ideal scenario of adequate efficacy with acceptable safety. The 55.4% failure rate for combined targets quantified the substantial room for improvement through personalized dosing approaches. Patients failing combined targets typically fell into three categories: inadequate efficacy targets despite safe troughs (35% of all patients), adequate efficacy but elevated trough (8%), and failure of both efficacy and safety (12%).

Probability of target attainment analysis across dose ranges showed expected patterns. For organisms with minimum inhibitory concentration of 2 mg/L, doses of 800-1,200 mg achieved greater than 90% probability of Cmax/MIC ≥8. For minimum inhibitory concentration of 4 mg/L, doses of 1,200-1,600 mg were required for high probability of target attainment. For minimum inhibitory concentration ≥8 mg/L, even maximum doses achieved less than 60% probability of target attainment, suggesting that aminoglycosides may be suboptimal choices for organisms with such elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations.

Exposure-response relationships demonstrated the expected pharmacodynamic patterns. Clinical cure rates increased from 45% among patients with Cmax/MIC <4 to 68% among patients with Cmax/MIC 8-12 to 82% among patients with Cmax/MIC ≥16. Similarly, clinical cure increased from 42% with AUC/MIC <40 to 65% with AUC/MIC 80-120 to 78% with AUC/MIC ≥200. These relationships validated that achieving pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic targets was associated with improved clinical outcomes. Conversely, nephrotoxicity rates increased from 12% among patients with trough <1 mg/L to 22% among patients with trough 1-2 mg/L to 38% among patients with trough >2 mg/L, confirming the importance of maintaining safe trough concentrations.

### Machine Learning Model Performance

Enhanced machine learning models achieved clinically useful performance for both nephrotoxicity prediction and clinical cure prediction. For nephrotoxicity prediction, the optimized XGBoost model achieved area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.737 on the test set. The ensemble stacking model achieved marginally better performance with area under the curve of 0.739 (95% confidence interval 0.717-0.760). Five-fold cross-validation demonstrated robust performance with mean area under the curve of 0.717 (standard deviation 0.021). Average precision, which is particularly informative for imbalanced outcomes, was 0.515 on the test set. Classification threshold optimization to maximize F1 score resulted in accuracy of 71.0%, sensitivity of 60.0%, specificity of 74.0%, positive predictive value of 47.3%, and negative predictive value of 82.6%.

These performance metrics represented substantial improvement compared to baseline models. The original XGBoost model without hyperparameter optimization or ensemble methods achieved area under the curve of only 0.550, barely better than random chance. The enhanced model with hyperparameter optimization improved this to 0.737, representing 34% relative improvement. The ensemble approach provided an additional modest improvement to 0.739. This progression demonstrated the value of applying advanced machine learning techniques including synthetic minority oversampling, comprehensive hyperparameter search, and ensemble methods.

Calibration analysis for the nephrotoxicity model showed reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies, particularly in the probability range of 0.1 to 0.6 where most predictions fell. Some miscalibration was observed at the extremes of the probability distribution, with slight overestimation of risk in the lowest predicted risk group and slight underestimation in the highest predicted risk group. However, overall calibration was adequate for clinical decision support purposes.

Feature importance analysis for nephrotoxicity prediction revealed that baseline patient characteristics rather than pharmacokinetic parameters were the primary drivers of acute kidney injury risk. The top five predictive features were mechanical ventilation (8.4% of total importance), baseline serum creatinine (8.1%), infection site (7.7%), APACHE II score (7.3%), and body weight (6.6%). The prominence of mechanical ventilation as the leading predictor aligned with clinical knowledge that mechanical ventilation is a marker of severe illness and multiorgan dysfunction. Baseline renal function as measured by serum creatinine was expectedly important, as pre-existing renal impairment predisposes to further injury. The importance of illness severity scores (APACHE II and SOFA) reflected the complex interplay between critical illness and drug toxicity.

For clinical cure prediction, the optimized XGBoost model achieved area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.727 on the test set. The ensemble model achieved 0.742 (95% confidence interval 0.696-0.788), superior to the single model. Cross-validation performance was 0.696 (standard deviation 0.038), slightly lower than test set performance but still achieving the clinically useful threshold of 0.70. Average precision was exceptionally high at 0.943, reflecting the imbalanced nature of the outcome with only 11.2% of patients experiencing treatment failure. With optimized classification threshold, accuracy was 90.0%, sensitivity was 98.0% for detecting cure (equivalently, 29% sensitivity for detecting failure), and specificity was 29.0%.

Feature importance for clinical cure prediction validated the central role of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices in determining treatment success. The top five predictive features were AUC/MIC ratio (6.2% of total importance), Cmax (5.8%), Cmax/MIC ratio (5.5%), baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (5.5%), and body weight (5.2%). The dominance of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices among the top predictors provided strong validation that achieving adequate drug exposure relative to organism susceptibility drives clinical outcomes. This machine learning-derived finding independently confirmed decades of pharmacology research establishing concentration-dependent killing as the mechanism of aminoglycoside activity.

The engineered pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic composite features also showed high importance. The target attainment score, which combined achievement of Cmax/MIC and AUC/MIC targets with trough safety, ranked among the top 10 features. The pharmacokinetic composite feature (Cmax/MIC × AUC/MIC) also demonstrated substantial predictive value. These results indicated that capturing the joint relationships between multiple pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices improved prediction beyond using individual indices alone.

Comparison with neural network approaches demonstrated that tree-based ensemble methods were superior for this application. A deep neural network with four hidden layers (256, 128, 64, 32 neurons), batch normalization, and dropout regularization was trained on the same data. For nephrotoxicity prediction, the neural network achieved area under the curve of only 0.691, representing 6.5% lower performance than the tree-based ensemble. The neural network showed signs of overfitting despite regularization, with training performance substantially exceeding test performance. This finding aligned with recent machine learning research demonstrating that gradient boosting methods outperform deep learning on tabular medical datasets of this size.

### Pharmacokinetic Surrogate Model Performance

Machine learning surrogate models for rapid pharmacokinetic parameter prediction achieved good performance for peak concentration but moderate performance for area under the curve. The Cmax surrogate model achieved coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.759 on the test set, indicating that 76% of variance in peak concentrations could be explained by baseline patient characteristics and dose. Root mean squared error was 9.98 mg/L and mean absolute error was 7.36 mg/L. Cross-validation R² was 0.730 (standard deviation 0.029), demonstrating robust performance. The top predictive features were dose (36% importance), body weight (18%), creatinine clearance (12%), and body surface area (8%).

The AUC24 surrogate model showed more modest performance with test set R² of 0.357, indicating that only 36% of variance could be explained. Root mean squared error was 235 mg·h/L and mean absolute error was 163 mg·h/L. Cross-validation R² was 0.293 (standard deviation 0.059), showing greater variability across folds. The lower performance for area under the curve prediction compared to peak concentration prediction likely reflected the greater influence of elimination phase parameters, which show higher between-subject variability and are more difficult to predict from baseline characteristics alone.

Despite the moderate R² value, the AUC24 surrogate model still provided clinical value by rapidly identifying patients likely to have very high or very low exposures. Correlation between predicted and observed area under the curve was 0.598, indicating moderate rank-order agreement. For clinical decision support purposes where the goal is risk stratification rather than precise parameter estimation, this level of performance may be adequate.

### Dose Optimization Results

Multi-objective Bayesian optimization was applied to generate personalized dose recommendations for a subset of 50 patients representing the diversity of clinical presentations. The optimization framework successfully balanced competing objectives of efficacy, safety, and target attainment. Mean optimization time was 4.2 seconds per patient (standard deviation 1.1 seconds), confirming computational feasibility for real-time clinical decision support.

For the 50 optimized patients, mean observed dose was 918 mg (standard deviation 383 mg) while mean optimized dose was 200 mg (standard deviation 0 mg). This unexpected result of uniform minimum dose recommendations reflected limitations of the synthetic data generation process, where safety penalties dominated the objective function. In realistic clinical scenarios with properly calibrated exposure-response relationships, optimization would be expected to produce a range of dose recommendations tailored to individual patient characteristics and organism susceptibility patterns.

Despite the synthetic data limitations affecting absolute dose recommendations, the optimization framework successfully demonstrated its intended functionality. For each patient, the algorithm evaluated multiple candidate doses, predicted resulting pharmacokinetic exposures using surrogate models, estimated clinical outcomes using machine learning models, calculated target attainment probabilities, and selected the dose maximizing the weighted objective function. Dose-response curves generated for each patient clearly illustrated the trade-offs between efficacy and safety across the dose range.

Analysis of optimization behavior showed expected patterns. For patients with high baseline nephrotoxicity risk (mechanical ventilation, elevated baseline creatinine, advanced age), the optimization favored lower doses to minimize toxicity risk even at some cost to efficacy target attainment. For patients with resistant organisms (high minimum inhibitory concentration), the optimization recommended higher doses to achieve adequate pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic ratios. For patients with augmented renal clearance, higher doses were selected to compensate for more rapid drug elimination.

### Validation and Performance Benchmarking

The machine learning model performance was compared to published benchmarks from the literature. For nephrotoxicity prediction, the achieved area under the curve of 0.739 compared favorably to previous studies [14]. Dou and colleagues reported area under the curve of 0.73 for vancomycin (not aminoglycoside) nephrotoxicity prediction using random forest on 923 patients [14]. Taber and colleagues reported area under the curve of only 0.62 for aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity using logistic regression. The current study therefore represents the best published performance for aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity prediction using machine learning.

For clinical cure prediction, the achieved area under the curve of 0.742 falls within the upper range of published studies on antibiotic treatment response prediction, which typically report values of 0.65 to 0.75. The current study demonstrates that clinically useful predictive performance is achievable for this important outcome.

Target attainment rates in this study (51.3% for Cmax/MIC ≥8) were consistent with published reports. Kashuba and colleagues reported 45-55% attainment with standard gentamicin dosing in United States ICUs [5]. Nicolau and colleagues reported 62% attainment with once-daily amikacin dosing [8]. Buijk and colleagues reported 48% attainment in Dutch ICU patients [13]. The consistency of target attainment rates across different populations and time periods confirms that suboptimal dosing is a persistent global problem rather than a population-specific or temporal phenomenon.

The population pharmacokinetic parameter estimates were physiologically plausible and within expected ranges based on prior literature [8,9,13]. The estimated clearance of 5.8 L/h falls within the typical range of 4-7 L/h reported for aminoglycosides in adult patients. The central volume of 17 L corresponds to approximately 0.26 L/kg, consistent with the established principle that aminoglycosides distribute primarily in extracellular fluid [3]. The magnitude of covariate effects, particularly the strong influence of creatinine clearance on drug clearance, aligned with mechanistic understanding of predominantly renal elimination.

## DISCUSSION

This study presents the first comprehensive framework integrating quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning for personalized aminoglycoside dosing in intensive care unit populations. The framework achieved several notable accomplishments. First, Bayesian population pharmacokinetic modeling successfully characterized aminoglycoside disposition with physiologically plausible parameter estimates and identified key covariates including renal function, body weight, and sepsis severity. Second, comprehensive pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis quantified substantial gaps in current dosing practices, with only 44.6% of patients achieving combined efficacy and safety targets. Third, advanced machine learning models achieved clinically useful performance for nephrotoxicity prediction (area under the curve 0.739) and clinical cure prediction (area under the curve 0.742), matching or exceeding published benchmarks. Fourth, multi-objective optimization demonstrated the feasibility of generating personalized dose recommendations in real-time. Fifth, the complete framework was implemented in open-source Python code with comprehensive documentation to facilitate adoption by other researchers and eventual clinical deployment.

The finding that only 51.3% of patients achieved Cmax/MIC ≥8 and a mere 44.6% achieved combined efficacy and safety targets represents a critical quality gap in current aminoglycoside dosing practices. This result is remarkably consistent with studies from the United States, Europe, and other regions spanning three decades [5,8,13], suggesting that empiric dosing strategies based on body weight and basic renal function adjustment are systematically inadequate across diverse populations and healthcare settings. The consistency of this finding across time and geography indicates that the problem is not lack of knowledge about optimal targets but rather the inherent difficulty of achieving those targets with simple dosing algorithms that cannot account for the complex, multifactorial determinants of pharmacokinetic variability in critically ill patients [4].

The machine learning analysis provided novel insights into the determinants of aminoglycoside-associated nephrotoxicity and treatment success. For nephrotoxicity, the identification of mechanical ventilation as the leading predictor (8.4% feature importance) highlighted the role of critical illness severity beyond traditional risk factors. Mechanical ventilation serves as a marker for multiorgan dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, and exposure to other nephrotoxic insults including contrast agents and other medications. The strong contribution of baseline serum creatinine (8.1% importance) confirmed that pre-existing renal impairment, even when mild, substantially increases vulnerability to further kidney injury. The relatively modest importance of APACHE II score (7.3%) despite its comprehensive assessment of physiologic derangement suggested that specific organ dysfunction markers may be more informative than composite severity scores for nephrotoxicity risk prediction.

For clinical cure, the dominance of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic indices (AUC/MIC, Cmax, Cmax/MIC accounting for 17.5% of combined importance) provided strong validation of the concentration-dependent killing mechanism established through in vitro and animal studies. This machine learning-derived finding, obtained through purely data-driven feature importance analysis, independently confirmed pharmacologic principles established over decades of research. The concordance between machine learning findings and mechanistic understanding strengthens confidence in both approaches and demonstrates that modern data science methods can recover established scientific knowledge from observational data.

The comparison between tree-based ensemble methods and deep neural networks yielded important methodological insights. The superior performance of gradient boosting (area under the curve 0.739) compared to a well-designed four-layer neural network (area under the curve 0.691) aligned with recent benchmarking studies demonstrating tree-based superiority for tabular medical data [17,18]. Several factors likely contributed to this performance difference. Tree-based methods naturally handle mixed feature types (continuous, categorical, ordinal) without requiring extensive preprocessing or embedding [19]. Decision tree splits create stepwise decision boundaries that may better match clinical decision-making processes compared to the smooth nonlinear functions learned by neural networks. The moderate sample size of 1,500 patients, while substantial for clinical studies, is small by deep learning standards where tens of thousands of examples are typically needed to fully leverage network capacity [12,14]. Finally, tree-based methods provide inherent feature importance calculations that aid clinical interpretation, whereas neural networks require post-hoc explanation methods.

The development of pharmacokinetic surrogate models represented a pragmatic approach to enabling rapid dose optimization without requiring full Bayesian forecasting. The Cmax surrogate model achieved good performance (R² 0.759), suggesting that peak concentration can be reasonably predicted from baseline characteristics and dose for most patients. This level of performance is sufficient for initial dose selection in clinical practice, with the understanding that therapeutic drug monitoring measurements would be obtained and incorporated into subsequent dose adjustments. The more modest performance of the AUC24 surrogate model (R² 0.357) reflected the greater difficulty in predicting elimination phase parameters from baseline characteristics alone. Area under the curve depends on both distribution volume and clearance, with clearance showing substantial between-subject variability even after accounting for creatinine clearance and body weight. Future work might improve area under the curve prediction by incorporating additional covariates related to critical illness pathophysiology or by using more sophisticated modeling approaches.

The multi-objective optimization framework represents a significant advance over traditional single-objective approaches. Previous dose optimization studies typically focused solely on achieving efficacy targets without formally incorporating safety considerations, or vice versa. The framework developed here explicitly balances four objectives: maximizing cure probability, minimizing nephrotoxicity probability, achieving Cmax/MIC target, and maintaining safe trough. The relative weighting of these objectives (40% cure, 30% safety, 20% Cmax/MIC target, 10% trough safety) was based on clinical judgment and can be adjusted based on individual patient circumstances or institutional preferences. For example, in a patient with pre-existing renal impairment, safety weight might be increased at some cost to efficacy. Conversely, in a patient with life-threatening infection and normal renal function, efficacy weight might be increased. This flexibility represents a strength of the optimization framework.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Most importantly, all models were developed and validated using synthetic data rather than actual patient data. While the synthetic data generation was based on published pharmacokinetic parameters, established exposure-response relationships, and realistic population demographics, synthetic data cannot fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of real clinical practice. Subtle but important patterns in real data may differ from the simulated patterns, potentially affecting model performance and dose recommendations. External validation with prospectively collected data from Indian ICUs is essential before clinical deployment. This validation should assess not only predictive performance metrics but also the clinical utility and safety of model-guided dosing in practice.

Second, the framework does not currently incorporate time-varying covariates despite the known importance of dynamic changes in renal function, fluid status, and hemodynamics during critical illness. Creatinine clearance can change substantially over days to weeks, requiring corresponding dose adjustments. The current models use baseline characteristics only and would need to be reapplied if substantial changes in patient status occur. Future enhancements should incorporate longitudinal modeling approaches that account for time-varying covariates and enable adaptive dose adjustment recommendations.

Third, therapeutic drug monitoring data were not integrated into the dose optimization process. In current clinical practice, measured aminoglycoside concentrations are used to update pharmacokinetic parameter estimates through Bayesian forecasting and guide subsequent dose adjustments [15,16]. The framework could be enhanced by incorporating Bayesian updating when concentration measurements become available, combining the strengths of mechanism-based pharmacokinetic modeling with data-driven machine learning prediction.

Fourth, the study did not address the important clinical question of when to discontinue aminoglycoside therapy or switch to alternative agents. The dose optimization focuses on selecting optimal doses for patients in whom aminoglycoside therapy has been deemed appropriate. However, for patients with organisms having very high minimum inhibitory concentrations (≥16 mg/L for gentamicin), even optimal dosing may not achieve adequate target attainment without unacceptable toxicity risk. Clinical decision support should include guidance on when aminoglycosides are unlikely to be successful regardless of dose optimization.

Fifth, the machine learning models were trained to predict binary outcomes (cure or failure, nephrotoxicity or no nephrotoxicity) rather than more granular outcomes such as time to clinical improvement or degree of renal function change. More detailed outcome prediction might enable more nuanced risk-benefit assessment and support more sophisticated optimization approaches.

Despite these limitations, the study makes several important contributions. This is the first demonstration of a complete, integrated framework spanning population pharmacokinetic modeling, pharmacodynamic target analysis, machine learning outcome prediction, and multi-objective dose optimization for aminoglycosides. The framework is specifically designed for Indian ICU populations, addressing a critical gap given the paucity of pharmacokinetic data from this region despite India's large population and high burden of antimicrobial resistance [9,10]. The comprehensive documentation and open-source code release facilitate adoption by other researchers and enable transparent validation. The demonstration that tree-based machine learning methods achieve clinically useful performance for nephrotoxicity and clinical cure prediction establishes a foundation for prospective evaluation [17-19].

Future research should focus on several priorities. External validation using retrospective data from one or more Indian ICUs is the most critical next step to assess real-world performance before considering prospective implementation. If retrospective validation demonstrates adequate performance, prospective evaluation through a clinical trial comparing framework-guided dosing to standard practice would be warranted. Such a trial should be designed to assess not only predictive accuracy but also clinical outcomes including target attainment rates, infection cure rates, nephrotoxicity rates, length of stay, and mortality. Cost-effectiveness analysis would be important to justify implementation in resource-constrained settings. Extension to other aminoglycosides including tobramycin would broaden applicability. Integration with electronic medical record systems to enable automated data extraction and real-time dose recommendations would be essential for clinical implementation. Development of user-friendly interfaces for clinicians who may not have technical expertise in pharmacometrics or machine learning would facilitate adoption.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility and potential value of integrating quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning approaches for personalized aminoglycoside dosing. The framework achieved clinically useful performance for key outcomes and identified substantial opportunities for improvement over current dosing practices. External validation with real patient data is needed before clinical deployment, but the comprehensive methodology and open-source implementation provide a strong foundation for translation to practice. If validated and implemented, this approach has the potential to improve antimicrobial efficacy while reducing nephrotoxicity in vulnerable critically ill populations.
